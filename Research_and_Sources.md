# One for You, One for Me

## Public research and decision brief

**Decision under review.** Can Crocs recruit the friend of an existing wearer as a customer for a small, personal gift? The proposal gives the buyer a choice of two eligible charms and space to explain the connection. The buyer can keep one or give both away. A graduation pilot in Singapore offers a specific occasion, a visible alternative and measurable retail economics.

### Purchase behavior and competing explanations

Crocs has shown a Korean-heart charm being given to another person in an entertainment-led monsoon campaign across seven APAC markets. This establishes a regional brand precedent for gifting. It provides no conversion rate or evidence that buyers would pay for a two-charm gift. [Campaign coverage][gift]

Fashion and accessories accounted for 27% of Southeast Asian video-commerce GMV in the 2025 e-Conomy SEA report. A short demonstration could make the gift understandable. The number describes the channel’s category mix. It cannot predict paid acquisition costs, the age of a buyer, or demand for charms. [Research report][sea]

**Price audit.** The Singapore Letter B listing showed S$6.95. Two comparable singles give a reference of S$13.90. The I Do five-pack listed at S$14.95. A separate letter configurator displayed S$5.95 each. All are public listing snapshots checked 20 November 2026. The S$13.90 figure is arithmetic from one SKU, not an observed price for a bowl charm or an offer from Crocs. Recheck eligible SKUs, stock, discounts and basket totals at the till. [Letter B][letter] · [Five-pack][pack] · [Configurator][letters]

An owner can restyle existing charms without buying anything. A giver can choose a themed pack, an unrelated present or a handwritten card. A positive reaction to the packaging could therefore coexist with weak incremental revenue. Neither the supper story nor the AI-generated product concept represents a real customer or a confirmed Crocs SKU.

### Consumer validation before rollout

Recruit an exploratory sample of 24 adults aged 18–29 in Singapore who can name a friend with compatible Crocs footwear. Include both owners and non-owners as potential buyers. Seek participants beyond Crocs stores to reduce enthusiast bias. Record consent and anonymized responses. The sample size is a proposed discovery target. No interviews have been conducted.

1. Ask about the last gift actually bought, its price and the occasion before showing the concept.
2. Let the buyer choose the same two eligible charms at the same verified till price either loose or on the proposed gift card. Rotate presentation order. Include their preferred alternative and the option to buy nothing. The wedding five-pack is a pricing reference only. Test actual available items.
3. Record the choice, the occasion and whether the person can pick two designs in under two minutes. Ask what they would otherwise buy and whether they would prefer to give both charms.
4. Count choices and report the denominator. Quote participants only with permission. Stated preference guides the pilot and cannot validate a sales forecast.

### Store experiment and measurement

Measure four selling stores and four similar comparison stores over a four-week baseline and ten selling weeks around graduation. Avoid mixing airport and neighborhood stores in a matched pair. Use the same eligible SKU prices. Use a graduation gift card at the entrance and a campus-group invitation subject to permission. Record the discovery channel at checkout. Train staff to record the gift offer with one POS code. Keep footfall, regular charm sales, returns, promotions and out-of-stock days by store-week.

Estimate lift by comparing each test store’s change in total charm contribution per week with its matched store’s change. Count eligible buyers who select a card and distinguish stand-alone charm purchases from shoe baskets. Offer anonymous single-question checkout feedback about the occasion. Analyze store-pair variability and promotion differences. With four pairs the result is directional. Repeat a second occasion if the estimate is unstable.

Use the model’s fixed spend and variable costs to judge payback. At the base assumptions the pilot needs 23 sets per selling store per week to recover all pilot spend. The forecast assumes 18 and therefore a loss of about S$1,013. Expansion is conditional on positive measured incremental contribution before fixed spend and a credible path to covering local rollout costs.

### Financial boundaries

See Financial_Model.xlsx for editable downside, base and upside cases in constant SGD equivalents. The model assumes net price S$12.75, variable product cost S$3 per pair, and additional card and handling of S$0.80 per gift. In the base case 35% of sales replace the purchase of the same two charms. Only the remaining 65% generate additional product margin. Packaging and handling apply to every gift. Fixed costs are incurred even at zero volume. Pilot budget of S$5,000 comprises proposed design and card setup S$2,000, training and displays S$1,500, and measurement S$1,500. The downside case assumes 30% of gift purchases are additional, with 10, 12 and 14 sets per store-week. Continuing the modeled rollout under those conditions loses S$29,490 over three years. A failed pilot triggers redesign or a stop. Later program budgets are illustrative envelopes. Unit costs, conversion, displacement and local price realization require validation. No online shipping, wholesale, footwear uplift, tax on profit or central overhead is included.
